Insight

Enormous time was lost every day to manual data cleaning — typos, misfielded entries, wrong terminology. The platform had no automation for work that was almost entirely mechanical. The early warning system fired reliably, but alarm conditions couldn't be combined, so every alert still required manual comparison and interpretation.

The Problem

Microbiologists and epidemiologists were monitoring national bacterial outbreaks using a system decades past its design date — and it was eating their time on work a computer should be doing.

Background

A national surveillance platform where manual bottlenecks had real public health consequences

SVEBar is the national platform used by microbiologists and epidemiologists across Sweden to monitor and report multiresistant bacteria. Early outbreak detection depends on this system working well.

The existing platform was decades old and showed it. The interface had no coherent UX. Incoming lab reports arrived with typos, misfielded data, and inconsistent terminology — and cleaning them was almost entirely manual. The early warning system fired when it was supposed to, but the conditions it could check were too simple. Alerts were a signal that still required expert interpretation before anyone could act.

This wasn't a cosmetic problem. Time spent on manual data work is time not spent on surveillance.

My Role

Solo designer embedded with the Public Health Agency team for the full engagement

UX/UI Designer at LH+P, working embedded with the The Public Health Agency of Sweden team. I was the only designer across the full engagement — running research, running workshops, building and testing wireframes, and handing off a design system aligned to public sector standards.

I had direct access to the epidemiologists and microbiologists who used the system. No researcher in between. What I observed went straight into design decisions.

Discovery

Observation first, then structured interviews — because watching sessions gave context that asking couldn't

I spent the first weeks watching before asking. Sitting in on actual monitoring sessions with microbiologists gave me a clearer picture than interviews alone would have. Then I ran workshops and interviews with microbiologists and epidemiologists across regional labs and national infection units — mapping the full workflow from a new positive sample arriving to an outbreak flag being raised.

1
Data quality work was consuming expert time every day

Incoming lab reports regularly contained typos, entries in the wrong field, and free-text terminology that didn't match the system's database. Fixing and categorising this was manual, repetitive, and unavoidable — scientists with deep domain expertise were spending hours on work that should have been automated.

2
Alarms were reliable — but too blunt to act on directly

The early warning system worked as designed, but it could only check simple conditions. Configuring combinations — e.g. a specific bacteria, above a threshold, in a specific region — wasn't possible. Every alert that came in had to be compared and interpreted manually before anyone could decide whether it meant something.

3
Navigation was deeply nested for common tasks

Routine configuration required multiple levels of navigation with no shortcuts and no visible state. Getting back to where you were meant starting over.

4
Synonym matching was a daily manual labour task

Lab submissions used free-text bacteria identification. Because terminology wasn't standardised across labs, scientists had to manually reconcile terms against a synonym database constantly. The database existed — working with it was painstakingly slow.

Baseline going in

The clearest signal was time. Scientists with deep domain expertise were spending meaningful parts of their day on mechanical data work — not because they lacked skill, but because the platform gave them no alternative. The redesign target was straightforward: automate what could be automated, and make the alarm system expressive enough that it could do more of the interpretation work for them.

The Hard Part

A regulated environment, expert users who couldn't describe their needs, and alarm logic that had to be powerful without becoming a configuration burden

Regulated public sector constraints

Modern and usable, but couldn't stray far from Swedish government design conventions. Every visual decision needed to be defensible within those standards.

Expert users with unstated mental models

The users knew exactly what they needed. They just couldn't always describe it in UX terms. Ethnographic observation became the research method — not surveys or questionnaires.

Designing alarm combinations without creating complexity

The value of combined alarm conditions only materialises if scientists can actually build them without needing technical help. The design had to make a genuinely complex feature feel approachable to users who are experts in bacteriology, not software.

Slow-moving institutional approvals

At a national public health agency, decisions on platform direction needed sign-off at multiple levels. Moving quickly meant figuring out what could ship independently of that process.

How I Navigated It

The navigation structure didn't match the monitoring mental model — so I restructured around how the work actually happens

Once I understood the real workflow end-to-end, the priorities became clear. The synonym matching problem was the most tractable: the database was already there, the bottleneck was the interface for working with it. A guided matching flow — where the system suggests likely matches as you type and you confirm or correct — could cut a slow daily task to almost nothing.

For the alarm system, the challenge was designing combinations that experts could build themselves. I worked through the real monitoring scenarios with the scientists — what conditions they actually wanted to watch for — and used those to define a condition builder that was expressive but not intimidating. The mental model was: add a filter, see the scope narrow, add another.

Underneath both of those: rebuilding the navigation around the monitoring workflow rather than the system's internal data model. Routine tasks should be reachable in two steps, not five.

How Might We

Make the alarm system expressive enough to do more of the interpretation work — so an alert is something you act on, not something you still have to manually assess?

Design

Dashboard-first architecture, a configurable alarm condition builder, and a synonym flow redesigned from scratch

With the architecture reframed around how monitoring actually works, I started designing. I built the dashboard, alarm configuration, and synonym flow in parallel — making sure the system felt coherent at every level of access.

1
Dashboard-based architecture

Replaced the deeply nested menu system. Primary view shows monitored bacteria, current status, and active alarms in one place — matching the real monitoring mental model rather than the system's internal data structure.

2
Configurable alarm condition builder

Step-based flow for building multi-condition alarms — bacteria type, threshold, region, time window, and more in combination. Designed so scientists could build meaningful, specific alerts without technical help. The more specific the alarm, the less manual interpretation needed when it fires.

3
Visual alarm overview with condition summaries

Active alarms display their configured conditions at a glance. Scientists can see exactly what each alarm is watching for without navigating back into settings — and can quickly judge whether an active alarm is relevant to act on.

4
Synonym management redesign

Replaced a slow, difficult database interface with a guided matching flow for mapping incoming lab terms to known organisms. The system suggests the most likely match as you type — you confirm or correct. Turned a task scientists dreaded into a one-click confirmation workflow.

5
Design system aligned to Swedish public sector standards

Where the standards didn't cover specific patterns — alarm priority logic, condition builder states — I documented proposed extensions with rationale, keeping them alignable with existing standards.

Testing & Iterations

Walkthrough sessions structured around the three biggest pain points identified in research

Wireframe walkthroughs with lab users and regional infection unit staff. Scenarios were built around the highest-friction moments identified in research: categorising an incoming lab report, setting up a multi-condition alarm, and acting on an active alarm.

Key validation: The synonym matching flow was the clearest win — participants moved through the guided flow significantly faster than the existing interface. The alarm condition builder was understood quickly once scientists saw how the scope narrowed with each condition added. The dashboard structure was immediately legible to everyone who tested it.

Results

Manual data work dramatically reduced — and alarms refined enough to mean something

↓ daily data cleaning time — synonym matching redesigned from slow manual lookup to guided one-click confirmation
0 nesting to reach monitoring status — dashboard replaced multi-level navigation
Multi condition alarms — scientists can now combine bacteria type, threshold, region and time window without technical help
1 handoff design system delivered to agency for ongoing development without a designer

What I'd do differently

Establish a quantitative baseline at the start. We knew the synonym matching and data cleaning work was eating time, but we didn't measure how much. A simple baseline — time-on-task for a typical daily report batch — would have made the before/after comparison concrete instead of qualitative.

Design System

Public sector standards applied and extended — with documented rationale for patterns the standards didn't cover

All work built to Swedish public sector design standards. Where the standards didn't cover specific patterns — the alert priority logic, the configuration summary states — I documented extensions with rationale, so the agency could keep building without a designer in the room.

The component library was built to be handed off, not just collected at the end. The agency needed to keep building after I left. Every extension included usage guidance and edge cases.

Looking ahead — the AI opportunity

The synonym matching problem I redesigned is exactly the kind of task AI autosuggestions are built for

The database was already there — what was missing was speed. A model trained on bacteriology terminology could surface likely matches as scientists type, turning a slow manual lookup into a one-click confirmation. The design pattern I built — guided matching with human validation — maps directly onto how AI-assisted workflows should be structured: the model suggests, the expert confirms.

That's the kind of AI integration that reduces friction without removing the human judgment that actually matters in public health surveillance.
